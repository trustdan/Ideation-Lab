# Challenge — expand into an adjacent mission or offering (Bet A)

This theme already carries two informal challenges logged as ideas —
[[30-sessions/2026-07-30-strategy-clinic/30-ideas/dot-vote-winners-may-reflect-excitement-not-buildability|the excitement-vs-buildability caution]]
and
[[30-sessions/2026-07-30-strategy-clinic/30-ideas/the-safer-bet-is-doubling-down-on-the-existing-prime|the safer-bet steelman]].
Both are accepted as read and are not repeated here. This note goes after assumptions
neither of them tested: not "is the vote count trustworthy" or "is a new bet worth the
capacity," but "is there actually a customer-side gap this bet can fill at all."

## Assumption inventory

1. The incumbent range integrator has an actual, unmet accreditation gap this company
   could fill — versus already having accreditation covered in-house or via an existing sub.
2. The target agency's cyber range modernization program will start budget planning "next
   fiscal year" as stated in the bet-brief — asserted, not verified against a public source.
3. "Sub first, prime later" is a real, walkable ladder in this market — not a story that
   makes a cold start sound less risky than it is.
4. The company's existing cyber accreditation discipline, built for its current
   agency/program, is recognizably transferable to a different agency's evaluators and
   environment (past-performance portability).
5. Senior BD time (one lead, part-time, 90 days) is actually available for this pursuit
   without cannibalizing Bet B's courting calls or whatever the flagship recompete needs —
   nobody has sized the BD bench against all three claims on it simultaneously.
6. A cold start in this market is solvable inside 60 days via a single teaming call — versus
   incumbent integrators in cleared/defense range work typically requiring far longer
   relationship-building before granting a real teaming conversation to an unknown entrant.
7. The dot-vote landslide reflects considered judgment about buildability rather than
   enthusiasm for the most novel-sounding idea in the room (flagged already, included here
   only because it compounds with #1 — an exciting idea with no confirmed demand is worse
   than either problem alone).

**Load-bearing:**

- **#1** — if the integrator has no real gap, or has already solved accreditation in-house,
  no amount of relationship-building creates an entry point; every downstream premortem in
  the bet-brief already assumes a gap exists without anyone having asked.
- **#2** — if the timeline is wrong, the bet is chasing a program that can't procure inside
  the horizon that matters, no matter how warm the relationship gets.
- **#6** — if 60 days is the wrong calibration for this market, the bet-brief's own kill test
  (2-week network probe, zero warm paths = kill) is miscalibrated in either direction: it
  could kill a genuinely viable pursuit too early, or count a single weak contact as a "pass"
  and send BD capacity chasing a false green light.

## Kill tests

- **#1:** Before running the network probe at all, have a technical lead with visibility
  into range-modernization or cyber accreditation work call one contact who knows the
  incumbent integrator's current staffing or scope, and ask directly: "do they have an
  accreditation gap, or is that function already covered?" One call, same week — cheaper
  than the network probe and tests demand instead of just access.
- **#2:** Pull the target agency's published budget justification / acquisition-forecast
  notice, or ask an existing cleared contact in that agency's contracting shop for the
  program's real timeline — a same-day desk check.
- **#6:** Ask two people in the network who've cold-started a subcontract relationship with
  an unfamiliar integrator recently: how long, typically, from first contact to a real
  teaming conversation? Recalibrate the kill test's own pass bar before trusting a "pass."

## Premortem — twelve months later, this failed

1. **Most probable:** the 60-day teaming call happens and is polite, but goes nowhere — the
   integrator was never actually short on accreditation capability, they were just being
   courteous — and a year later there's still no teaming agreement, matching the safer-bet
   steelman's prediction almost exactly, just confirmed by direct experience instead of
   argued from principle.
2. **Second:** a warm path is found and the kill test technically "passes," but the contact
   turns out to be low-influence inside the integrator's org — BD capacity gets committed on
   a technically-true but meaningless green light, and the actual gatekeeper never responds.
3. **Third:** the agency's budget planning does start next fiscal year as hoped and a real
   gap exists, but the incumbent integrator's own prime contract vehicle doesn't structurally
   allow adding new subs mid-period — a contract-mechanics kill nobody checked because
   everyone was focused on relationships, not procurement structure.

## Reframes

- Instead of "how do we get a teaming call," ask: **what does the incumbent integrator's
  current team actually lack, and would they tell us before we're a threat to them** — get a
  real demand signal before spending any relationship capital.
- Invert the entry ladder: rather than "sub first, prime later" through one incumbent's
  goodwill, HMW find a smaller, faster foothold in the same mission area that doesn't depend
  on a single gatekeeper — e.g., a direct small task order in cyber accreditation elsewhere
  in the same agency?
- Reframe the dot-vote process itself: HMW re-score next quarter's shortlist with "evidence
  of existing relationship or demand signal" as an explicit weighted criterion alongside raw
  dots, so a landslide can't hide a cold start again?

## Steelman of the opposite

Set beside Theme 1's Bet B specifically (not the general "defend the flagship" case already
on file): every open question about Bet A — does the integrator have a gap, does the
agency's budget line up, does the contract vehicle even permit adding subs — is answerable
only by someone else's undisclosed plans. Every open question about Bet B — does the "never
no-bid" pitch hold up, will the courted prime take a call — is answerable largely by this
company's own action and its own records. A rational actor forced to bet scarce BD hours on
exactly one of these before knowing anything else should prefer the bet whose uncertainties
it can resolve unilaterally over the one whose uncertainties depend entirely on a stranger's
procurement calendar. That the room voted 11-to-9 in the opposite direction of
verifiability is itself worth saying out loud, not just noting as a live tension.

## Verdict

**reshape** — run the cheap demand-check kill test (#1) before spending any of the 2-week
network-probe cycle on Bet A; if the integrator has no real gap, the cold-start problem was
never the actual risk.
